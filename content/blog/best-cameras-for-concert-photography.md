Concert photography is the hardest test you can give a camera: almost no light, then suddenly way too much, colors that change every half second, artists that never stand still, and no flash allowed. A camera that feels great in daylight can completely fall apart in the pit.

I photograph concerts and festivals for clients like North Sea Jazz, MOJO and Radio 538. My own kit is a pair of Canon EOS R5 Mark II bodies with f/2.8 zooms — and this guide is based on what actually survives that environment, not on spec sheets.

## What actually matters in a concert camera

Forget megapixels. In the pit, four things decide whether you get the shot:

- **High-ISO performance.** You'll live between ISO 3200 and 12800. A camera that's clean at 6400 is worth more than any other spec on this list.
- **Autofocus in low light and backlight.** Modern eye-AF has changed concert photography, but systems differ enormously once the smoke machine kicks in and the only light is a red backlight.
- **Buffer and dual card slots.** The chorus of song two is not the moment to hit a full buffer. Dual slots are non-negotiable when you're shooting for a client.
- **Silent shutter.** For seated venues, theaters and jazz clubs, a fully silent electronic shutter is the difference between being invited back or not — but check how it handles banding under LED stage lights.

Everything else — weather sealing, battery grip, IBIS — is nice, but these four decide your keeper rate.

## The best cameras for concert photography

### Best overall: Sony A7 IV

The [Sony A7 IV](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=sony+a7+iv) hits the sweet spot: a 33MP full-frame sensor that stays usable at ISO 12800, class-leading eye-AF that locks on through haze and backlight, dual card slots and a deep native lens ecosystem (including excellent third-party glass from Sigma and Tamron, which matters for your budget).

### Best in extreme low light: Sony A7S III

Only 12MP, but that's the point: the [A7S III](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=sony+a7s+iii) has the cleanest high-ISO files of anything in this price range. If you shoot small clubs with three PAR cans for a light show — or you deliver video alongside stills — this is the specialist's choice. For festival main stages with proper light rigs, the A7 IV's extra resolution is more useful.

### Best for Canon shooters: Canon EOS R6 Mark II

The [R6 Mark II](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=canon+r6+mark+ii) is Canon's concert workhorse: superb subject tracking, excellent high-ISO files from its 24MP sensor, and up to 40fps electronic shutter for catching the exact top of a jump. If you already own EF glass, the adapter works flawlessly, which makes switching cheap.

**What I actually shoot:** its bigger brother, the [Canon EOS R5 Mark II](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=canon+eos+r5+mark+ii) — two of them, so I never swap lenses mid-song. The stacked 45MP sensor gives me room to crop for editors, the fast readout means no LED banding in silent mode, and the AF simply does not let go of a singer moving through haze and backlight. To give you a real-world reference: my shots of Kane at Ahoy were taken on the R5 Mark II with the RF 15-35mm f/2.8L at ISO 2500, f/2.8, 1/800s — clean files, straight from the pit. Is it overkill if you're starting out? Absolutely. That's why the R6 Mark II is the recommendation and this is the upgrade path.

### Best for Nikon shooters: Nikon Z6 III

The [Z6 III](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=nikon+z6+iii) brought a partially stacked sensor to the mid-range: faster readout means less rolling shutter and less LED banding in silent mode — a genuinely underrated feature for theater and jazz work. Nikon's low-light AF has caught up with the best.

### Best on a budget: a used Sony A7 III

Here's the honest truth this far into the mirrorless era: a used [Sony A7 III](https://www.mpb.com/en-us/search?q=sony%20a7%20iii) from a reputable used dealer like MPB costs a fraction of a new body and will still outperform the photographer holding it. Every photo in my early portfolio was shot on gear older than this. Spend the difference on a fast lens — it matters far more.

## Spend your money on glass first

If your budget forces a choice between a better body and a faster lens, take the lens. An f/1.8 prime on a mid-range body beats an f/4 zoom on a flagship in the pit, every single time. The classic concert kit:

| Lens | Why |
|---|---|
| 24–70mm f/2.8 | The workhorse — 80% of pit shots |
| 70–200mm f/2.8 | Festivals, big stages, drummer shots |
| 35mm or 50mm f/1.8 | When the light gets truly bad |

For what it's worth: my own bag carries the full f/2.8 trinity, but in the pit the workhorses are the 15-35mm and 70-200mm on two bodies — wide for the barrier and full-stage shots, tele for faces and the drummer, no lens swaps mid-song. I'll cover concert lenses in depth in a separate guide, and the complete kit is in [What's In My Bag](/blog/whats-in-my-bag-concert-photographer).

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need full frame for concert photography?**
No — but it helps. The one to two stops of high-ISO advantage over APS-C are exactly the stops you're missing in a dark club. If you're starting on APS-C, shoot with fast primes and you'll be fine.

**How many megapixels do I need?**
20–33MP is the sweet spot. Editors rarely need more, files stay manageable at 2,000 shots per festival day, and lower-resolution sensors are usually cleaner at high ISO.

**Is the electronic shutter safe under stage lighting?**
Mostly, but test it: cheap LED fixtures cause banding with slower sensor readouts. Stacked and partially stacked sensors (A9 series, Z6 III) barely suffer from this; use the mechanical shutter when in doubt and silence isn't required.
